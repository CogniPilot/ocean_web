# Great white swimming and turning

The great white is a lamnid. During steady swimming, most of its visible lateral motion should build toward the peduncle and caudal fin. A compliant joint behind the head can help it shape a turn. Its bend should be small during a straight, fast stroke, and the turn should come from hydrodynamic forces on the moving body and fins.

## Anatomy and stiffness

The Sharks4Kids skeleton illustration shows a continuous vertebral column reaching from the back of the head into the upper tail lobe. The Shark Trust poster places the gills behind the head, the pectoral fin base below that region, and the main dorsal fin farther aft. Those landmarks are useful for rigging. The model can represent the gradual curvature of many vertebrae with one additional yaw coordinate across the anterior trunk. The skull, jaw and eyes should move together with that section during swimming. The jaw can still articulate for feeding. [Sharks4Kids skeleton and muscle](https://www.sharks4kids.com/shark-skeleton-muscle), [Shark Trust anatomy poster](https://www.sharktrust.org/storage/752/shark_anatomy_a3_poster_web.pdf)

Knaub and colleagues used micro-CT to compare vertebral shape and mineral architecture across six lamniform species. Their white shark sample comprised 11 centra from four adults. The white shark and shortfin mako had similar centrum geometry in several comparisons. White sharks had more mineralized lamellae toward the tail and a shortened posterior centrum near the precaudal pit. The scans describe the structure that must transmit load while allowing movement. Deriving a spring constant from these data would require joint geometry, soft-tissue properties and mechanical loading measurements. [Knaub et al., *Journal of Anatomy*](https://doi.org/10.1111/joa.70209)

Porter and colleagues measured axial strain directly in the vertebral columns of swimming spiny dogfish, then measured comparable strains in excised segments under bending. This supports including restoring torque and damping in a reduced-order shark spine. A study of mineralized cartilage in several elasmobranchs also found species-dependent mechanical properties. Treat the bending and damping coefficients as model parameters to fit from motion. [Porter et al., *Built for speed*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24388493/), [Porter et al., *Material properties and biochemical composition*](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.02325)

## Swimming regimes and turning

| Behavior | Evidence | Use in the simulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine swimming near a seal colony | Propeller speed sensors recorded individual modal five-minute speeds of **0.80–1.35 m/s**. One shark also sustained about **2 m/s** at the surface for an hour. Tagged animals often glided during descents. [Watanabe et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.185603) | Calibrate routine swimming near 1 m/s, include a faster travel case, and confirm that a descending glide can persist with little or no tail motion. |
| Steady lamnid propulsion | Swim-tunnel video, muscle measurements, and dissections in shortfin mako showed that force transmission and body kinematics concentrate propulsive motion toward the tail. Comparative dissections traced the relevant tendon architecture. [Donley et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02435), [Gemballa et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16429422/) | Check a rising lateral displacement envelope along the body and rearward progression of the bend. Use the mako data for the shape of the envelope and white shark tags for speeds. |
| Predatory ascent | Seal Island tags recorded ascent from depths up to 20 m over roughly **7–16 s**, with about **30°** additional pitch. Predatory ascents had dominant tail-beat frequencies of **0.83–1.67 Hz**, compared with around **0.4 Hz** in ordinary ascents. Reported peak frequency was **2.50 Hz**, with a maximum **estimated** speed of **6.5 m/s**. [Semmens et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00227-019-3542-0) | Calibrate the buildup, pitch and power reserve independently from the steady stroke. Ocean Web's current 15 m/s launch target is a gameplay setting. |
| Short attack burst | A separate tag and camera study reported **3.3 Hz** and **6.7 m/s estimated** in one filmed seal attack. [Watanabe et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3354/meps12981) | Use as a short-duration comparison. The speed estimate applies to that observation. |
| Routine turns in the wild | Drone tracks show variable movement, from relatively straight beach travel to searching and circling. Tag data from 21 white sharks show substantial variation in turning and activity across habitats and life stages. A 2026 Cape Cod preprint reports **4–10 m** circling radii in seven tracks. [Colefax et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2020.00268), [Jewell et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.4825), [Cape Cod preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.19.739271) | Compare matched, low-speed circles. Keep the preprint range provisional. Track radius alone cannot resolve the joint motion or establish a high-speed turning limit. |
| Fin action in turns | Bonnethead and Pacific spiny dogfish experiments documented asymmetric pectoral rotation during yaw turns. In fin-rotation analyses, inside-fin depression was associated with turning performance. Leopard shark measurements link pectoral motion to the initiation of pitch changes. [Hoffmann and Porter, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obz014), [Hoffmann et al., 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10107303), [Wilga and Lauder, 2000](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/bio_facpubs/11/) | Use qualitative evidence for independent fins and coordinated bank. White shark fin angles and force coefficients require calibration. |

## Baseline and validation

I ran the existing learned controller against the local preview's actual HarborTraffic WebAssembly. With no current, a 2.4 m/s initial forward speed and a 0.55 effort command, the left and right quarter turns took **8.34 and 8.59 s**. Travel distance divided by the 90° change in course yielded effective radii of **13.53 and 13.85 m**. The existing trunk flex reached **13.0° and 12.0°**. This test measures movement direction, including sideslip. The Cape Cod tracks cover a different speed and behavior, so replication at comparable cruise speed is needed before using the radii to tune the controller.

Use the same Modelica joint coordinate to drive the bow crossflow strip, contact geometry and skinned mesh. Check left and right turns at matched starting speeds, then compare settle time, radius, speed loss and peak curvature. For straight swimming, measure mean forward speed, stroke frequency, lateral excursion by body station and yaw oscillation. Measure path curvature from the course in the water frame over a rolling stroke period. Use a separate glide and breach test to catch changes to buoyancy, pitch or sustained propulsion. Repeat the open-water measurements with the baseline and learned controllers before comparing their training checkpoints. The field studies constrain the behavior more tightly than they constrain exact hinge angle, stiffness or damping.

The tuned anterior-joint candidate completed the same learned turns in **7.73 and 7.99 s** with **12.42 and 12.76 m** effective radii. Peak anterior joint yaw was about **14°** in those turns and **1.33°** in the straight run at the same effort. Mean straight-swim speed was **1.97 m/s** for both builds to two decimal places. At 1.15 m/s initial speed with the lower effort command, the deterministic controller's effective radius changed from roughly 12 m to **10.6–10.8 m**. This remains a model fit. It passes a relative steering test and still needs matched-speed field calibration, especially for the amplitude and phase of the stroke along the centerline.
