# Where the Modelica sources live

Ocean Web owns its browser FMU boundaries, vehicle and world assembly, and geometry generated from its scene assets. The source build pins CogniPilot's modelica_models. Change shared physics in that library and update the pin in build.sh. The small local entry points preserve the names used by Rumoca, browser manifests and benchmarks.

### Piccard electrical load

Piccard uses an assumed six-cell, 10 Ah LiPo pack (22.2 V nominal) and a regulated 16 V thruster bus. The installed thruster model is unconfirmed. The simulation interpolates forward and reverse current separately from the September 2019 Blue Robotics 16 V bollard test over the simulated ±14 N range. The simulated pack resistance (45 mΩ), converter efficiency (94%), hotel load (18 W), pump losses, dry-rotor load and resting voltage curve are estimates. The pack supplies the motors and pumps until its modeled usable charge is exhausted. [Manufacturer’s T200 specifications](https://bluerobotics.com/store/thrusters/t100-t200-thrusters/t200-thruster-r2-rp/) · [Original performance workbook](https://cad.bluerobotics.com/T200-Public-Performance-Data-10-20V-September-2019.xlsx)

### Piccard rotational dynamics

The 18 kg dry mass is a simulation assumption. The model allocates 3.6 kg to the paired side plates, 2.4 kg to the battery and tray, and 0.344 kg to each thruster assembly. It assigns the remaining mass to the loaded barrel. The principal moments come from these dimensions and offsets. Submergence adds rotational inertia estimated by distributing the transverse added masses across the frame. Ballast and flooding change the moments separately.

The damping combines low-speed resistance, rotational cross-flow drag and water moving past the vehicle. The model uses the relative water velocity and applies the resulting torque at the body. Real Piccard mass distribution, angular acceleration under known torque and unpowered coast-down data would allow calibration of these estimates. Simulated release and piloted-turn results are in [PERFORMANCE.md](PERFORMANCE.md#piccard-rotational-response). The formulation follows marine rigid-body and added-mass dynamics. The BlueROV2 experiments illustrate the uncertainty in estimating rotational coefficients for another open-frame vehicle. [Fossen’s marine craft model](https://www.fossen.biz/html/marineCraftModel.html) · [von Benzon et al., sections 4.2–4.3](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/10/12/1898)

### Human diver locomotion

The ScubaDiver FMU advances a floating torso and ten massive limb segments with fourteen joint axes, using the MultiBodyLie articulated-body solver. The head and equipment are rigidly attached to the torso. FMI inputs can replace the built-in gait with bounded joint commands. The resulting torques are net joint efforts. Muscle activation, fatigue and tendon compliance require further modeling.

#### Water loads on the articulated diver

The torso, head, tank, buoyancy jacket, thighs, shanks, boots, upper arms and forearms have separate immersion and displacement calculations. At each site the free surface cuts a fitted ellipsoid. The volume below that plane determines lift, and the centroid of that volume determines its moment. Vest inflation changes the displaced volume at the jacket. Six points per drag site sample the water-relative velocity, including velocity from the torso and each ancestor joint. The model sums direction-dependent quadratic drag and its moment about the supporting link. The fin blades and palms have separate hydrodynamic surface loads. The torso envelope includes the jacket’s drag. The formulation follows segment-based swimming simulation and marine rigid-body mechanics. [Nakashima et al., 2007](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jfst/2/1/2_1_56/_article) · [Fossen’s marine craft model](https://www.fossen.biz/html/marineCraftModel.html)

Displacement is trimmed for an equipped mass of 88 kg in seawater of density 1,025 kg/m³. The fully immersed model distributes an assumed 17 kg of isotropic translational added mass across the links, scaled by immersion. Segment areas and drag coefficients are engineering estimates. The approximation omits wake interaction, directional added mass, and flexible blade deformation. The FMU exposes passive water force and moment per link in world coordinates, dissipated power by link, and active blade and palm loads separately. Independent coast-down, stroke-force and freeboard measurements are needed to calibrate the parameters.

#### Evidence for swimming and rolling

| Experiment | What it measured | How to use it here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ten divers with eight fin designs | Pendergast and colleagues measured kick frequency, speed, maximal thrust and oxygen consumption. The power and recovery strokes contributed unequally, and fin stiffness changed the observed stroke. [Pendergast et al., 2003](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/evaluation-of-fins-used-in-underwater-swimming/) | Fit fin thrust against cadence and amplitude. Check the signed impulse in each half-stroke. Fitting mean speed alone lets errors in drag cancel errors in thrust. |
| Instrumented diving fin and captured diver motion | Nakashima and colleagues identified bending and fluid-force coefficients experimentally. They fitted the equipped diver’s drag to mean speed. At a 1.65 s cycle their model gave 0.51 m/s and 0.091 m/s peak-to-peak speed variation. The experimental fluctuation was also 17.8% of mean speed. [Nakashima et al., 2018, §§2–3](https://doi.org/10.3390/proceedings2060288) | Replay the measured joint trajectories with the same starting conditions. Use the within-cycle speed variation as an independent check after fitting the mean. A matching cadence alone leaves kick amplitude undetermined. |
| Pool-mounted robotic legs with two actual fin types | Johnson and Goldfarb varied hip excursion from 10° to 40° peak to peak. They recorded joint motion, torque and thrust under 35 combinations of fin and stroke. Their fixed-base loading differs from a freely swimming diver’s. [Johnson and Goldfarb, 2024, §§2–4](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0263574724001231) | Isolate a pair of fins on a stationary fixture. Compare phase-resolved thrust and joint work on unseen stroke amplitudes and frequencies. This separates fin calibration from the body drag and swimming controller. |
| Human flutter kicks at two speeds | Yang and colleagues recorded motion and eight muscle EMG signals in ten divers. Faster trials increased peak joint velocities, increased ankle excursion and shifted ankle timing. Ankle antagonist muscles showed strong coactivation. [Yang et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42259155/) | Compare hip–knee–ankle phase lags and ankle stiffness across speeds. The current joint-torque output cannot establish muscle activation or co-contraction. |
| Divers with different gear placement | Pendergast and colleagues found changes in the location and density of equipment could raise energy cost by 30% in slow swims. A separate experiment recorded three kick styles with and without diving equipment. [Pendergast et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-199605000-00006) · [Rejman et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0236504) | Measure a submerged glide with the actual tank, vest and ballast before tuning active swimming. Track centre of mass and displaced volume as gas changes. Keep fin work, whole-body metabolic power and regulator gas flow as distinct quantities. |
| Hand sculling and body roll | Takagi and colleagues measured unsteady forces during sculling. Yanai measured the effect of buoyancy on long-axis roll in competitive front crawl at the surface. Neither experiment establishes the roll rate of a fully submerged diver wearing a tank. [Takagi et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25310026/) · [Yanai, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15046989/) | Log signed moments from each fin, hand and buoyancy separately. Record roll rate and coast-down after a single stroke with an equipped diver. Test both roll directions and repeat with the hands quiet. |

#### Helicopter turn

During an underwater helicopter turn, the leg on the turning side tucks, opens and extends in a frog kick. The opposite leg feathers on extension and draws water forward with a back kick. In an eight-second still-water test the simulated diver turned about 75° each way. Integrating the body-frame loads measured opposing fore–aft impulses and yaw impulses of the same sign from both fins. Run `node scripts/check-scuba-fins.mjs` to repeat the maneuver. A matched trial with an equipped human diver is still needed to validate turn rate.

#### Reproducing a reference cadence

Run `node scripts/measure-scuba-gait.mjs` to sample the shipped diver FMU at 100 Hz in deep, still water. The test records mean speed and peak-to-peak speed variation during the third stroke at the 1.65 s reference cadence, and mean speed during the last six seconds of ordinary and sprint trials. The published fin model reported 0.51 m/s and 0.091 m/s at that cadence. Our controller changes kick amplitude with cadence. A numerical fit requires the measured joint motion, fin stiffness and equipment drag. [Nakashima et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/proceedings2060288)

#### Walking, wading and human inertia

De Leva provides segment masses, centre-of-mass locations and radii of gyration relative to anatomical landmarks. Those measurements provide a starting point for scaling the body. The diver’s cylinder and ballast need their own mass, position and displaced volume. The articulated solver exchanges linear and angular momentum between the moving limbs and torso using the fitted segment masses, centres and principal inertias. Rajagopal et al. provide a higher-fidelity comparison model with 37 degrees of freedom and 80 lower-limb muscle–tendon units, along with validation against human gait. Their walking validation applies to their land model. [De Leva, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8872282/) · [Rajagopal et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27392337/)

Zhu et al. recorded synchronized kinematics and ground reactions for 20 adults walking on solid ground and sand. Their sand trials had longer stance and reduced push-off. Grant et al. published stride timing and foot-sinkage data spanning different sands. Human shallow-water gait measurements also found reduced joint torques at chest depth. Those results support separate tests for dry firm sand, yielding sand and wading. Each sole in the simulator currently supplies compliant normal support and friction. A steady weight check cannot reveal an incorrect toe-off impulse, a blade tip intersecting the ground, or excessive balance assistance. [Zhu et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39927884/) · [Synchronized walking dataset](https://doi.org/10.17632/jgdpjrf584.2) · [Grant et al. dataset](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.pc866t1w0) · [Human joint loading in shallow water](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021929011000479)

#### Validation before transferring a learned controller

1. Record the diver and gear configuration, fin dimensions and flex curve. Fit passive translation and rotation from coast-down trials, fin loads from a fixed-base test, and centre of pressure and slip from foot contact. Record uncertainty when two parameter combinations explain the same data.
2. With those parameters fixed, replay unseen cadence and amplitude combinations. Report force in newtons, joint work per cycle in joules, and forward speed in metres per second, including the time histories and phase error. During a roll, compare signed angular impulse and actual rotation about the body axis. The integrated Euler roll angle is unreliable near vertical pitch.
3. Compare walking speed, stance fraction, fore–aft impulse, peak normal load and **blade-tip** clearance across dry sand and wading speeds. The studies above do not supply the exact maneuver of walking a beach in long scuba fins while carrying a cylinder. A synchronized side view and force measurement of that configuration would resolve this gap.
4. Hold out people, fin models and ballast configurations during validation. Establish acceptance bands from measured variability and instrument error. Train over supported parameter uncertainty and report held-out results separately. A policy that succeeds in the browser currently demonstrates control of this simulated diver.

## Herring gull flight and paddling

The flight model uses a floating torso, two shoulder–wrist chains and two hips. Twelve spanwise sections see the local air velocity from ground motion, moving joints, bank and the harbour wind field. A horizontal wind gradient distinguishes a gust hitting one wing first. The tail uses the flow at its own position. The harbour supplies horizontal wind, so vertical gust structure would require another environmental input.

Harvey (2024) found that joint extension speed affects the available longitudinal response in gull-inspired glides. Wu et al. (2025) measured pigeon wing trajectories and investigated how sweep changes forces during a wingbeat. These results motivate independent inner and outer wing motion and upstroke folding. The simulated gait and shaft limits are estimates for this rig. [Gull joint extension study](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2023.0734) · [Pigeon wing kinematics](https://doi.org/10.3390/biomimetics10050328)

Each section carries slow and fast Wagner circulation states, a lagged separation fraction and a filtered normal velocity for apparent-mass forces near reversal. The sections share a mean downwash correction. Boutet and Dimitriadis describe the underlying indicial response and a more detailed spanwise lifting-line solution. Section polars, feather twist, water-entry effects and living herring gull muscle torques still require species-specific measurements. [Wagner lifting-line formulation](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/5/3/92)

The feet use hip motion, ankle angle and web spreading to derive local velocity and projected area in water. Immersion and pressure drag set the force at the web, its hip reaction and its turning moment about the torso. These are reduced-order paddle forces. Joint and fluid states travel with the animal during multiplayer host transfer.

## Manta pectoral locomotion

The manta has a floating trunk and two independently actuated pectoral hinges. For the 5.5 m, 420 kg simulated animal, the model assigns 336 kg to the central disc and 42 kg to each fin. Segment mass fractions, centres and inertias are estimates. MultiBodyLie couples the three bodies, including the reaction of the trunk to wing acceleration. Water-relative foil, drag and lift loads act on each wing. Dry flight advances the anatomical centre of mass under gravity. Direct hinge efforts and direct fin-control inputs are available through FMI, along with joint angles, rates, muscle torques and power.

Fish et al. recorded nine swimming mantas and found different upstroke and downstroke kinematics. Their numerical study placed most streamwise propulsion on the distal half of each wing, with appreciable spanwise and chordwise deformation. We represent that motion using a hinge and a first-order delay in thrust from the moving fin. This is a reduced-order approximation of distributed bending and the wake. The 3.5 and 7 m/s control settings are simulation targets. Calibrating the model to a particular animal would require its mass distribution and fin motion at measured speeds. [Fish et al., 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/3/3/20)

## Great white shark turning

Animal-borne camera footage records white sharks making tight turns during prey pursuits. In bonnethead sharks, tracked turns link body curvature and pectoral fin motion to changes of direction. The latter provides a mechanism to test across species. It does not provide measured white shark joint limits or muscle stiffness. [White shark camera observations](https://connectsci.au/wr/article/53/10/WR25072/275839/Through-the-camera-lens-a-window-into-the) · [Bonnethead turning kinematics](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obz014)

The simulator recruits a coupled rear-trunk and peduncle bending mode during a hard turn. A passive end-range spring resists excessive peduncle motion. Local flow over the bent rear body, caudal fin and paired pectorals generates loads and turning moments. The compliant range and muscle gains are modeling assumptions. Run `node scripts/check-shark-turn.mjs` to measure elapsed time and effective radius for a 90° change in the direction of travel on both sides from 2.4 m/s in still water.

## Package structure for multiple fidelities

The proposed public catalog gives each supported vehicle or animal one stable family name. The domain libraries supply reusable components. An example composes a particular plant, controller, environment and task. The catalog namespace is a design choice for this repository. The Modelica Standard Library uses both reusable components and complete system examples, including RobotR3. [Modelica Standard Library RobotR3](https://doc.modelica.org/Modelica%204.0.0/Resources/helpDymola/Modelica_Mechanics_MultiBody_Examples_Systems_RobotR3.html)

A possible root is `Systems`, with families such as `Watercraft.Hobie16`, `Aircraft.Rdd2`, `Robots.Microduck` and `Animals.GreatWhite`. These are proposed names. Existing library paths remain live. For example, a Hobie family could contain the following classes:

| Relative class | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Interfaces.PartialPlant | Shared physical interface with units, ENU and body-frame conventions, control limits, initial conditions and observations. |
| Models.Polar | Fast maneuver prediction within the measured sailing polar. |
| Models.Multibody | Hull and rig dynamics, control actuators and instrument outputs. |
| Controllers | Helms which declare the inputs and actuators they require. |
| Examples | Runnable compositions such as a tack in shifting wind and a paired controller race. |

A scenario can select a replaceable plant constrained by the family interface, or replace a single subsystem such as a sail or hydrodynamic load model. Put common geometry and mass properties in shared parameter records. Expose additional capabilities through explicit interfaces when a detailed plant supports a maneuver or sensor absent from the reduced plant. Keep physical fidelity, numerical solver, CPU/GPU backend and rendering detail as independent choices.

A scored evaluation should record the concrete plant and component redeclarations, parameter set, controller and checkpoint, task version, weather seed, solver, step size and numeric precision. Publish records for the same event and evaluation configuration together. This allows a controller to train against a faster model and compete against a fixed reference model. The existing Hobie course-cost predictor accepts headings and forecasts and returns a loss. It is not a drop-in simulation plant.

During migration preserve existing public class paths through short aliases and retain thin browser FMU names where saved controllers depend on them. The upstream Marine.Sailing.Hobie16 and Ocean Web's Hobie16 have different dynamics and controls. Explicit comparison of interfaces and trajectories is required before sharing a single implementation.

The reference shape, body-local attachment frames, collision surfaces and asset licenses belong with the supported model in its Modelica library. Use a package resource URI such as `modelica://Marine/Resources/Shapes/Hobie16/Hull.glb` for a portable visual asset. A physics variant can reference its own collision mesh and inertia data while several variants share one visual mesh. Record units, axis conventions, mesh-to-body transform and mesh revision. Rendering LOD, shaders and scene effects belong to the consuming viewer. Keep a headless build free of rendering dependencies. [Modelica package resources](https://specification.modelica.org/maint/3.6/packages.html#external-resources)
